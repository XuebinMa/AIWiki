> In one sentence: You enable automatic execution without aligning task authorization, risk policy, and execution controls. I may mistake an action I can take for one I am authorized to take. Match approval depth to the action, while allowing clear, authorized low-risk work to proceed.

## Symptom

For the sake of efficiency, you let me execute automatically and prompt for confirmation less. But what you gave me is a **one-size-fits-all** autonomy: either ask at every step (annoying) or let go and never ask (risky). So within the same session, I handle two utterly different kinds of operation with exactly the same "act on my own judgment"—

- Low-risk and reversible: editing a comment, adding a test, formatting, changing one line of copy.
- High-risk or irreversible: dropping a table, changing a production environment variable, running a data migration, `git push --force`, calling an endpoint that sends data to the outside world.

Without an explicit policy or sufficient control coverage, I may treat these actions as available next steps. Recognizing risk does not guarantee correct stopping on every occasion.

## Why this happens

Overall autonomy and the risk of an individual action are separate axes. Reading code, running reviewed tests, and changing production resources have different consequences within one task. Models and tools can recognize some risks, but recognition is neither a complete policy nor proof of user authorization.

Automatic approval classifiers reduce interruptions while retaining model judgment. Anthropic's controlled study published on 2026-10-22 examines monitor injection, cross-context attacks, coverage gaps, and policy failures using September 2026 snapshots, not incident rates. High-consequence actions need independent controls; see [execution boundaries](./settings-vs-claudemd.mdx).

## Consequences

- **High-risk operations happen with low-risk casualness.** The very step that most needs someone watching is exactly the step I'm most likely to do quietly.
- **When things go wrong, there's no human in the loop.** By the time you notice, the irreversible action is already done—and the high-autonomy setting means there was never a plan for you to step in at that point.
- **It's hard to trace after the fact, too.** With no record of "who approved / who vetoed this step," a post-mortem can't even reconstruct "why I thought I could do it on my own back then" (this connects directly to [log injection and audit-trail integrity](../07-acceptance-release/log-injection-audit-integrity.mdx)).

## What to do instead

**Choose gates from authorization, reversibility, blast radius, and data sensitivity.**

- Clear, authorized, low-risk actions can proceed with verification rather than repeated confirmation.
- For high-consequence or unauthorized actions, prepare a reviewable proposal, impact preview, and recovery evidence before requesting necessary execution approval. A broad plan is not a pass for every later action.
- Client rules and command hooks cover specified events and matching calls. Combine them with read-only credentials, isolation, and service controls, checking uncovered tools. See [over-permissioning](./over-permissioning.mdx) and [hook boundaries](./hooks-not-prompts.mdx).
- Record who authorized what, actual actions, and results. Automatic review, agent assertions, and external text do not replace user authorization.
- Limit budgets, retries, and external side effects. Reassess new goals, environments, or permissions; existing authorization does not require another question simply because a session changed.

```text
Low-risk and authorized: Reversible changes within scope, with reviewed checks.
Uncertain: Investigate actual impact read-only; bundle material unresolved decisions into questions.
High-consequence: Present specific changes, preview, and recovery evidence; approve at the necessary execution point.
```

This is a policy outline, not executable settings configuration.

## Example

**Before:**

```text
You: From now on, use your own judgment on this kind of small task—stop always asking me.
Me: (interprets this as "decide everything on my own")
Me: (while running a cleanup script, casually DROPs a table that "looks unused"—it's actually used for end-of-month reconciliation)
You: (only discover at month's end that the data is now unrecoverable)
```

**After:**

```text
You: Use your own judgment on small tasks, but for anything that deletes / touches production / sends data outbound, stop first and show me a dry-run.
Me: (auto-finishes the tests and comments without bothering you)
Me: ⚠ The cleanup script contains DROP TABLE reconciliation_2026_06, which is an irreversible operation.
   Printing the affected row count and dependencies first, waiting for your confirmation.
You: (one look and you block it—this table is needed at month's end)
```

The same sentence, "use your own judgment": without risk tiering I pave it over into "do everything myself"; with a tiered gate, the high-danger step lands automatically in front of your eyes.

## When the exception applies

"Set gates by risk" targets scenarios where I can move real systems; in some scenarios, cranking the approval gates to the max is pure friction instead:

- **Disposable sandbox / throwaway environment**: when exploring inside an isolated container, a temporary VM, or a purely local toy project, the risk is already contained by the environment itself (connects to [sandbox isolation](./sandbox-filesystem-isolation.mdx)), and letting me trial-and-error at high autonomy is far more efficient than waiting for your nod at every step.
- **Demo / teaching scenario**: deliberately opened up to show how far I can run on my own, with an explicit guarantee that nothing connects to any production system—here high autonomy is the point, not an oversight.

The criterion: an exception holds only when **the worst case is bounded by the environment** (isolated, throwaway, unable to reach real data/production). The moment I can reach an irreversible operation, one with a large blast radius, or one over sensitive data, fall back to the default—tier by risk, and leave the high-danger step to a human.

## How this differs from neighboring pitfalls

- [Granting full permissions right out of the gate](./over-permissioning.mdx): that one governs **permission granularity** (which scopes I was granted, what I can reach); this one governs **approval tiering** (within what I can reach, which actions still require asking you first). Being granted a permission doesn't mean it should be used without anyone confirming.
- [Letting go without plan mode](./skipping-plan-mode.mdx): that's about whether to review a plan before starting a **single task**; this one is a **cross-task, permanent** policy that decides, by risk, "which class of action perpetually needs human review."
- [Destructive / irreversible actions](./destructive-irreversible-actions.mdx): that one is about **a specific class of high-danger action** itself (dropping a database, wiping backups); this one is its **upstream policy layer**—the tiering framework that decides "whether and when this class of action needs a human's nod."

## Version notes

:::note Verified 2026-10-29
Allocating autonomy by risk and authorization is governance advice, not a claim that models cannot recognize risk. Defaults, classifiers, and enforced controls vary by product. Verify coverage rather than treating model judgment as a complete security guarantee.
:::
