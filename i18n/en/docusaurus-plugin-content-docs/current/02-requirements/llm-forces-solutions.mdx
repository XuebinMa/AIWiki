> In one sentence: when a requirement is missing key information, you expect me to stop and ask. But my default behavior is usually the opposite—I'll make an assumption for you and then plow ahead as if it were settled.

## Symptom

You give me a requirement: "Build an export feature for the user list." In your head, you haven't actually decided: CSV or Excel? Paginated or not? How are permissions handled? You assume I'll clarify these. But usually I won't—I just pick CSV, export everything, add no permission check, and hand you a "complete" implementation. By the time you notice the direction is wrong, the work is already done.

## Why this happens

With missing information or tools, I may still generate calls or implementation as if prerequisites were satisfied. The issue is failing to recognize, verify, or handle a consequential gap, not proof that training rewards necessarily discourage questions.

The study cited by the original engineering article alters APIBank/ToolBench instances to remove tools or input details. It observes unsuitable tool use in tested models and evaluates structured verification. **This supports checking information/tool sufficiency, not a claim that all current coding agents force solutions in real projects**.

Use authorized repository and documentation access first, then classify remaining gaps by impact. Ask about consequential decisions that cannot be established independently; disclose assumptions and proceed on ordinary reversible details.

## Consequences

- I deliver a whole implementation built on a wrong assumption; the rework costs far more than asking one question up front would have.
- The assumption is buried in the code—you may not see at a glance what I "decided" on my own.
- The vaguer the requirement, the more room I have to improvise, and the higher the chance of going off course.

## What to do instead

1. **Check prerequisites first.** Inspect the task, previous answers, repository contracts, and available tools instead of asking you for discoverable facts.
2. **Identify consequential blockers.** Clarify undefined format, data scope, or authorization when they change implementation. Disclose project-consistent assumptions for low-cost reversible details.
3. **Continue independent work.** While awaiting a critical answer, perform authorized reading, checks, or implementation that does not depend on it, without pretending the unknown is settled.
4. **Scale planning to the task.** Use a short specification and acceptance criteria for complex work; complete small fixes directly. Require handling consequential gaps rather than stopping for every unknown.

## Example

**Before:** I export all users without checking existing authorization and arbitrarily choose CSV.

**After:**

```text
Me: The existing export interface defines department isolation and a row limit; I will reuse them.
    CSV versus Excel remains undefined and affects the caller, so clarify that format.
    Meanwhile I can inspect shared data/authorization paths. Button styling follows existing
    components rather than becoming another blocker.
```

Once the consequential decision is clear, complete the implementation and report actual verification, without promising that clarification guarantees first-try success.
