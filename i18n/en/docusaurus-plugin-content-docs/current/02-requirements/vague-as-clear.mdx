> In one sentence: when your requirement has ambiguity, gaps, or several reasonable readings, I most likely won't stop to ask — I'll quietly pick one and build it out. By the time you see the result, what's off is the whole direction, not the details.

## Symptom

You say: "Add me a search box that searches users."

That one line hides at least four things I can't pin down from the words alone: search by username, by email, or both? Fuzzy matching or exact? Search-as-you-type or only on Enter? How do I show an empty result? Hand the same sentence to a different reader and you'd get completely different, equally reasonable answers.

By rights I should surface those and ask you. But my usual move is to pick "exact match on username, triggered on Enter," then hand you a search box that works. I won't flag that "I just made four decisions for you here." On the surface I delivered cleanly; in reality I turned at four forks in the road without signaling once.

## Why this happens

A request such as "search users" can have several reasonable meanings: fields, matching, and triggering change the result. I may adopt one without exposing the choice. A confident tone proves neither internal certainty nor that I recognized every ambiguity.

This entry concerns **choosing between viable interpretations**. [Implicit assumptions](./implicit-assumptions.mdx) concerns unstated premises; [missing prerequisites](./llm-forces-solutions.mdx) concerns insufficient execution inputs. They can coexist without making every unknown a blocker.

Check existing requirements, interfaces, and UI conventions first. Clarify remaining ambiguity that changes the goal or causes consequential outcomes, while disclosing reversible display choices and proceeding under project conventions.

## Consequences

- **The rework is directional, not cosmetic.** When the wrong assumption lives at the requirements level, my entire implementation is built on a bad premise. Changing a button color is cheap; tearing up "what we search and how" and redoing it is expensive.
- **The ambiguity gets "resolved" into thin air.** Once I commit to one reading in code, that fork disappears from the conversation. You don't see "there were four possibilities here" — you see a seemingly complete product, and it's hard to spot at a glance which calls I made for you.
- **The vaguer the requirement, the wider I drift.** More blanks means more room for me to improvise, and a higher chance the result strays from what you meant — which you often don't catch until acceptance.

## What to do instead

- **Check existing semantics first.** Read search contracts, authorization, and UI behavior and reuse settled answers.
- **Expose real branches.** Explain the consequences of undefined prefix/substring or username/email choices. Bundle consequential questions that change results or design.
- **Proceed on disclosed reversible details.** Reuse conventions for empty-state text and component styling. Mark low-cost unsettled choices as provisional rather than requesting repeated confirmation.
- **Check interpretations with acceptance examples.** Inputs that should match or fail to match, authorization, and empty-input results are more checkable than verbal confidence. Plan mode can assist complex tasks but does not replace contracts and validation.

## Example

**Before:** I silently choose exact username matching; only after delivery do we discover you wanted username/email substring search.

**After:**

```text
Me: The existing page defines search-as-you-type and the empty state; I will reuse both.
    Username, email, or both—and prefix versus arbitrary substring—remain undefined.
    These affect exposure, queries, and indexes, so clarify those semantics.
    Meanwhile I can inspect existing interfaces/authorization and use the existing UI components.
```

Verify the agreed interpretation with specific inputs and expected outputs rather than treating one confident answer as alignment.

## When the exception applies

Authorized exploration can present alternatives or a reversible prototype first. Routine details can follow conventions with disclosed assumptions. Consequential semantic disagreements still need shared decisions even if you have not selected an answer. Judge impact, authorization, and evidence rather than whether a unique answer already exists in your head.
